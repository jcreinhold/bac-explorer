Body-fat formula & sources
r ≈ 0.732 × (1 − body fat ÷ 100) ÷ Fw

First estimate fat-free mass, then assume 73.2% of that mass is water. Dividing estimated body water by whole-blood water content gives the apparent dilution volume; dividing by body weight gives r.

These published references are 0.825 for male study groups and 0.838 for female groups. Either can be selected; they are assumptions, not blood measurements. At a fixed body-fat value, the alternate gives an r about 1.6% lower.

Fat-free-mass hydration supports the 0.732 convention; Maskell et al., Eq. 2 gives the body-water-to-r conversion. Combining them is a derived approximation, not an independently validated athlete BAC formula.

Body-fat measurement error, dehydration, fluid retention, and variation in fat-free-mass hydration can shift r. Athlete research found similar average hydration of fat-free mass in athletes and non-athletes, with individual variation. Being an athlete alone does not determine r. What does r mean?

Alcohol spreads mainly through body water. Muscle contains more water than fat, so differences in muscle and fat help explain why the traditional male average is higher than the female average. Individual body composition can differ from either preset.

r is an effective dilution volume per kilogram. At r = 0.68, each kg of body weight contributes 0.68 modeled liters. Multiply r by weight in kg to get the model’s total volume. “Apparent” means inferred from alcohol dose and blood concentration, rather than a measured anatomical space. It is not your blood volume or body-water percentage.

At the same weight, more body fat generally means less dilution and a lower r. The optional body-fat method estimates r using explicit hydration and blood-water assumptions. It does not measure your personal r; do not enter your fat-free fraction directly as r.

Why use a fixed BAC drop?

Concentration drop and grams removed are different. For the same grams removed per hour, a larger dilution volume gives a smaller BAC drop. But grams removed also vary with lean mass, liver size, food, genetics, health, and drinking history; weight and r alone cannot determine them.

The default 0.015 percentage points/hour is a research-based population average for moderate drinkers. It stays independent and editable rather than being inferred from weight or r. Keeping it fixed while changing weight or r implicitly changes the grams/hour shown above. The allowed range is for comparison, not a personal confidence interval.

What do “hours” and “peak” mean here?

Hours means the total time from the first drink to the end of the drinking session. A row is the total number of standard drinks consumed during that time, not drinks per hour. The 0-hour column is a theoretical all-at-once, fully absorbed comparison; it is not a BAC immediately after a sip.

The table uses a simple dose-minus-elimination model to approximate a concentration around the end of a session. It assumes full absorption, enough alcohol present for elimination throughout the interval, and a constant elimination rate. It does not model when each drink was taken.

Your actual peak can occur during the session or after the final drink. Food, drinking pace, delayed absorption, body composition, medicines, health conditions, and metabolism can change the result substantially. A low estimate after many hours can especially mislead if most drinks were consumed near the end.

Do not use this table as a timer for driving, a drinking target, or a way to rule out alcohol overdose.

Formula, assumptions & source notes

A simplified Widmark-style model, in grams and kilograms:

BAC (%) = max(0, A / (10 × r × W) − β × t)
A
Alcohol dose: standard drinks × 14 grams.
W
Body weight in kg (lb × 0.45359237).
r
Effective dilution volume per kg: the model’s liters per kilogram available to spread out the dose. Multiply by weight to obtain the apparent distribution volume. Presets: 0.68 and 0.55 L/kg; r is not a body-water percentage.
β
Assumed fall in BAC concentration per hour, in percentage points (default 0.015), not grams of alcohol removed.
t
Total hours spent drinking.

The factor 10 converts g/L to g/dL, the usual US numeric % BAC convention. “Apparent” volume is an effective dilution space inferred from dose and blood concentration. Subtracting 0.015 per hour means a drop of 0.015 percentage points, not a 1.5% relative drop.

How the elimination units connect: if V = r × W liters and the body removes E grams/hour, then β = E ÷ (10 × V). Conversely, this app’s selected β implies E = 10 × β × r × W. At 160 lb, r = 0.68, and β = 0.015, V is about 49.4 L and E is about 7.4 g/hour. Those are model implications, not separate measurements.

Optional body-fat derivation: fat-free mass = W × (1 − body fat ÷ 100); estimated body water ≈ 0.732 × fat-free mass; r ≈ body water ÷ (W × Fw). The 0.732 coefficient and the 0.825 / 0.838 blood-water references come from separate research. Their combination is an assumption-based model, not an independently validated individual BAC equation.

A fixed concentration-drop average is a simplification, not a claim that clearance is unrelated to body size. Studies link grams/hour to lean mass and liver size. The app cannot infer an individual’s metabolic capacity from weight and r, so it keeps β independently adjustable and shows the implied grams/hour.

The model assumes no alcohol was already in the body before this session. These empirical population assumptions do not determine your personal distribution volume or clearance. The model ignores absorption delays and cannot reconstruct an individual BAC curve or its peak. No uncertainty interval is shown because these inputs cannot support a reliable individualized interval.

Color markers, legal limits & safety

The defaults are 0.040% and 0.080% BAC. The first is a rough “buzzed” reference, not an established onset threshold: people feel alcohol differently, and impairment can occur below 0.040%. The bands compare against your chosen markers; they do not diagnose intoxication or establish driving safety.

For context, 0.08% BAC is a widely recognized US driving reference, but laws vary by jurisdiction, age, and license type. Impairment and legal consequences can occur below that value. The default second marker happens to match that familiar reference; the colors still represent personal caution markers, not jurisdiction-specific driving limits. Never decide whether to drive from an estimate.

If someone is difficult to wake, has slow or irregular breathing, seizures, or other suspected alcohol overdose symptoms, seek emergency help immediately. Do not wait for a calculated number.
